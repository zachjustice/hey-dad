Fact-Check: Are Republicans Worse Offenders Than Democrats When It Comes to Election Interference?

October 24, 2026 · United States · Nonpartisan evidence review

Claim

"While both parties engage in election interference—including gerrymandering, voter roll purges, changing voting requirements, signature checks, ballot lists, etc.—Republicans are the worse offender."

Verdict: Mostly supported, with important qualifications

The evidence supports the conclusion that Republicans have been responsible for more extensive efforts to restrict voting access and have recently gained greater advantages from partisan redistricting. However, Democrats also engage in partisan gerrymandering, and not every election-security measure constitutes improper interference.

The distinction matters: election interference can mean deliberately manipulating election outcomes, but routine election administration—such as maintaining voter rolls, verifying signatures, or enforcing eligibility requirements—is not inherently improper.

There is also no universally accepted, comprehensive measure of which party commits more illegal election interference. The strongest evidence concerns restrictive voting legislation, partisan redistricting, and documented attempts to change election administration.

Key Findings

IssueWhat the evidence shows
GerrymanderingBoth parties engage in it; Republicans have generally benefited more in recent redistricting battles.
Voting restrictionsRepublican-led states have been the principal drivers of recent restrictive voting legislation.
Voter roll purgesRoutine maintenance is necessary; improper removals can disenfranchise eligible voters.
Voter ID and eligibility rulesLegitimate security objectives exist, but particular requirements may create unnecessary barriers.
Signature verificationErrors and unequal rejection rates occur, including in Democratic-led states.
Overall comparisonEvidence favors the claim in significant categories, but does not establish that every Republican policy is improper or quantify all misconduct across both parties.

Articles, Reports, and Primary Sources

Bias classifications describe general institutional perspectives, not whether individual findings are accurate. Nonpartisan does not mean entirely free of methodological or editorial judgments.

1. How Gerrymandering Tilts the 2024 Race for the House ↗

Brennan Center for Justice | 2024 | Left-leaning (voting-rights advocacy)

The Brennan Center estimated that congressional maps entering the 2024 election gave Republicans an advantage of approximately 16 House seats relative to its fair-map benchmark. It acknowledged Democratic gerrymandering but concluded that Republican maps, particularly in the South and Midwest, produced the larger overall advantage.

2. AP Analysis: Partisan Advantage in U.S. House Districts

Associated Press | August 2025 | Nonpartisan / Center

AP found that the 2024 House results were unusually proportional nationally: Republicans won 220 seats and Democrats 215, with state-level partisan advantages largely offsetting one another. Republicans benefited notably in Texas, Florida, North Carolina, and Wisconsin, while Democrats benefited in California, Illinois, and other states; this qualifies claims that Republicans maintained a large net national map advantage in 2024.

3. Midterms: Your Questions, Answered

Reuters | October 23, 2026 | Nonpartisan / Center

Reuters reported that redistricting ahead of the November 2026 elections favored Republicans in approximately 15 congressional districts across nine states, compared with six districts favoring Democrats. These are potential advantages, not guaranteed election outcomes, but they offer recent evidence that the latest redistricting battle has favored Republicans.

4. State Voting Laws Roundup: 2025 in Review ↗

Brennan Center for Justice | January 2026 | Left-leaning

The report documented 32 restrictive voting laws enacted in 17 states during 2025, as well as eight laws classified as creating risks of partisan interference in election administration. The findings demonstrate the scale of restrictive legislation, although raw law counts do not measure how many voters were affected or prove that every restriction was intended to suppress votes.

5. Republicans Won the Redistricting Battle ↗

Associated Press | June 2026 | Nonpartisan / Center

AP reported that Republicans could potentially gain 16 House seats from redistricting enacted across eight states, while Democrats could gain approximately six from maps in California and Utah. The report also documents Democratic efforts to redraw maps for partisan advantage, illustrating that both parties participate, although Republican efforts have been more extensive in this cycle.

6. Maryland Judge Rules New Congressional Map Unconstitutional ↗

Associated Press via CBS News | March 2022 | Nonpartisan / Center

A Maryland judge struck down a congressional map drawn by Democratic lawmakers, finding that it constituted extreme partisan gerrymandering under state law. This is a documented example of Democrats manipulating electoral district boundaries for partisan benefit, rather than merely responding to Republican actions.

7. Voter List Maintenance ↗

MIT Election Data + Science Lab | Updated May 2026 | Nonpartisan / Academic

MIT explains that removing deceased, relocated, or otherwise ineligible voters is a necessary election-administration function governed by federal safeguards. More than 21 million registrations were removed between the 2022 and 2024 general elections, but that figure alone provides no evidence of partisan misconduct; improper removals must be distinguished from legitimate maintenance.

8. Supreme Court Revives Virginia's Voter Roll Purge

Reuters | October 2024 | Nonpartisan / Center

Virginia's Republican administration removed roughly 1,600 registrations identified as potentially belonging to noncitizens; lawsuits argued that the process violated federal timing restrictions and mistakenly removed some citizens. The Supreme Court allowed the removals to proceed before the election, illustrating both the legitimate interest in removing ineligible voters and the risks of inaccurate or last-minute purges; its emergency ruling did not conclusively resolve every underlying legal question.

9. United States v. State of Alabama ↗

U.S. Department of Justice | 2024–2025 | Government / Institutional

In October 2024, a federal court temporarily halted Alabama's voter-removal program after the Justice Department challenged its legality under federal election law. In March 2025, the incoming administration dismissed the lawsuit, emphasizing the state's obligation to remove ineligible voters; the changing federal position highlights why actual court findings should be distinguished from partisan administrations' arguments.

10. Voter ID Laws ↗

National Conference of State Legislatures | June 2025 | Nonpartisan

NCSL reported that 36 states requested or required some form of voter identification, with substantial differences in the strictness of their requirements. Supporters emphasize fraud prevention and public confidence, while opponents question access burdens; these laws cannot all be classified as election interference merely because they impose verification requirements.

11. Strict Voter Identification Laws, Turnout, and Election Outcomes ↗

National Bureau of Economic Research | 2019 | Nonpartisan / Academic

Researchers studying Florida and Michigan found that strict identification requirements were unlikely to substantially affect overall turnout or change many election outcomes under their modeled assumptions. This challenges blanket claims that voter ID requirements necessarily produce large-scale partisan suppression, although the findings are not a comprehensive assessment of every state's laws or their unequal effects on particular groups.

12. 2024 Election Administration and Voting Survey ↗

U.S. Election Assistance Commission | June 2025 | Government / Nonpartisan

The federal survey found that signature problems were the most common reported reason for rejecting mailed ballots in 2024, accounting for approximately two-fifths of rejections. This demonstrates that signature verification has real consequences for eligible voters, but national rejection totals alone do not establish which political party bears greater responsibility.

13. UCLA Study Finds Disparities in Mail Ballot Rejections ↗

UCLA Voting Rights Project | September 2026 | Academic / Voting-rights-oriented

Researchers found disproportionate mail-ballot rejection rates among younger and non-White voters in California and Washington, with experimental evidence suggesting that subjective signature-review decisions can contribute to disparities. Because these problems occur in Democratic-led states as well, unequal treatment in election administration cannot reasonably be characterized as exclusively Republican.

14. Ballot Order Effects ↗

MIT Election Data + Science Lab | 2022 | Nonpartisan / Academic

MIT summarizes evidence that candidates appearing first on a ballot may receive measurable electoral advantages, sometimes large enough to affect close races. Since states use different ballot-order methods—including party-based arrangements, alphabetical ordering, and randomization—ballot design presents a genuine fairness concern, but this research does not establish which party exploits it more frequently.

Important Qualifications

Three distinctions are necessary for an objective conclusion.

First, intent, legality, and impact are not interchangeable. A voting regulation can be lawful but burdensome, politically motivated but lawful, or unlawful without proof of intentional discrimination. Similarly, an unequal electoral result does not, by itself, prove deliberate manipulation.

Second, the metric changes the answer. The Brennan Center's estimate of a 16-seat Republican structural advantage entering 2024 differs from AP's finding of a nearly proportional nationwide House result in 2024 because they use different benchmarks. Neither finding should be presented as an exact count of illegally manipulated seats.

Third, some categories lack reliable party-by-party comparisons. Research documents significant disparities involving voter-roll maintenance, signature verification, ballot design, and eligibility requirements, but there is insufficient comparable national evidence to declare one party the greater offender in every category.

Conclusion

The claim is mostly supported, particularly regarding voting-access restrictions and recent partisan gerrymandering.

The evidence indicates that Republican-controlled governments have more extensively pursued restrictive voting legislation, and Republicans gained the larger prospective advantage from the redistricting efforts preceding the 2026 midterms. Nevertheless, Democrats have also engaged in deliberate partisan gerrymandering, and documented administrative problems occur in states governed by both parties.

The most defensible conclusion is that Republicans have been the larger contributors to several major forms of partisan electoral manipulation in recent years, but neither party has a clean record, and the evidence does not justify labeling every election-security measure as interference.

That distinction is important for evaluating both parties using consistent standards rather than partisan assumptions.
