Voting Access & Election Interference Since 2017: A Reading List

Oct 24, 2026 · @Zach Jusice

How to use this list

Both parties have manipulated election rules since 2017, but the documented record is not symmetrical: most restrictions on casting a ballot (purges, ID and mail-voting limits, polling-place cuts) and the effort to overturn the 2020 result came from Republicans, while Democrats' record is concentrated in partisan gerrymandering and ballot-access fights.

  • Scope. Actions since January 2017 that make it harder for eligible people to vote or that weaken their vote's weight: gerrymandering, roll purges, polling-place changes, ID/mail rules, efforts to overturn results, and keeping candidates off ballots.
  • Not everything here is illegal. Roll maintenance is required by federal law, and most gerrymanders are legal. Each entry says what courts or analysts found, so the reader can judge.
  • Source mix. Wire services (AP), mainstream outlets, court rulings, nonpartisan academics, and advocacy groups on both sides. Advocacy sources are labeled so a skeptical reader can weigh them; where possible, a conservative or right-leaning outlet is included for the same fact.
  • Tip for the conversation. Leading with the Democratic section and agreeing those cases were wrong tends to make the Republican section easier to hear.

Election interference from Republicans

The Republican record since 2017 spans every category in scope: roll purges, registration holds, polling-place and drop-box cuts, mail-voting limits, mid-decade gerrymanders, a federal push for states' voter files, and a documented attempt to reverse the 2020 result. One entry (NC-9) is outright ballot fraud by a GOP operative. Newest first.

Florida mid-decade map (2026)

Gov. DeSantis drew and the legislature passed a new map aimed at adding up to four Republican seats, moving Florida toward roughly 24–4. DeSantis said it reflects population growth and removes race-based districts; a voting rights group sued.

Sources: UPI · Fox News · WUSF

DOJ demands for state voter files (2025–26)

The Justice Department sought full, unredacted voter files from nearly every state and sued 29 states plus DC that refused. Some judges, including a Trump appointee, rejected parts of DOJ's legal theory; Utah's Republican lieutenant governor also declined.

Sources: DOJ · Washington Times · Deseret News

Texas mid-decade redistricting (2025)

At Trump's request, Texas redrew its congressional map mid-decade to gain about five Republican seats. North Carolina, Missouri and Ohio followed, and Democratic states responded.

Sources: AP via Fox10 · Axios · Scripps News

Proof-of-citizenship executive order (2025)

An executive order sought to require documentary proof of citizenship to register and to reject mail ballots arriving after Election Day. Federal courts blocked it, ruling the president lacks authority to set those rules.

Sources: States Newsroom · TNND

North Carolina congressional map (2023)

After the state supreme court flipped to a Republican majority, it reversed its own earlier ruling against a GOP gerrymander. The legislature then replaced a 7–7 court-drawn map with one favoring Republicans in 10 of 14 seats.

Sources: AP via NHPR · NPR · Fox News

Alabama congressional map (2021–23)

The Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that Alabama's map likely violated the Voting Rights Act by limiting Black voters to one opportunity district. The legislature passed another map that still did so, and a federal court ordered a special master to redraw it.

Sources: Ballotpedia · CBS News · Encyclopedia of Alabama

Florida congressional map (2022)

DeSantis vetoed the legislature's map and pushed through his own, splitting a North Florida district that had elected Black representatives for three decades. A state judge struck it down, but appeals courts and a federal panel upheld it.

Sources: Florida Phoenix · AP via KVIA · Democracy Docket

Ohio congressional map (2021–22)

The Ohio Supreme Court twice struck down Republican-drawn maps as unconstitutional partisan gerrymanders, with the Republican chief justice joining the majority. Because of election deadlines, the invalidated map was still used in 2022.

Sources: AP via News 5 Cleveland · WOSU · Ballotpedia

Texas SB 1 mail-ballot rules (2021–22)

The law required ID numbers on mail ballots that match registration records. In the March 2022 primary, 12.4% of returned mail ballots were rejected, versus 0.8% in 2020; rejection rates were similar for both parties' voters.

Sources: CBS News · AP via Fox 26 · KUT/NPR

Georgia SB 202 (2021)

The law shortened the absentee-request window, cut Fulton County drop boxes from 38 to 8, gave the legislature control of the State Election Board chair, and allowed unlimited voter challenges. It also added a second required Saturday of early voting, and a federal judge found no proof of racial intent for several provisions.

Sources: WCNC explainer · Emory Wheel · GOP sponsor testimony

Efforts to overturn the 2020 result (2020–21)

Trump asked Georgia's Republican secretary of state to "find 11,780 votes" after three counts confirmed his loss, and his campaign organized alternate elector slates in several states. Both led to state charges in 2023.

Sources: National Review · WSB-TV · NPR

January 6 and pressure on Pence (2021)

Trump pressed Vice President Pence to reject or delay certified electoral votes, a power Pence and his counsel said he did not have. Pence refused, and a mob stormed the Capitol during the count; Congress certified the result that night.

Sources: CBS News · JURIST · AP via WPTV

Texas drop-box limit (2020)

Weeks before the 2020 election, Gov. Abbott limited each county to one mail-ballot drop-off site, cutting Harris County (4.7 million people) from 12 to 1. An appeals court let the order stand.

Sources: NPR · NPR via KPBS

Florida felon voting fees (2019)

After 64% of voters restored voting rights to most people with felony records, the legislature required all fines, fees and restitution be paid first. The state had no central record of what people owed.

Sources: Courthouse News · American Oversight

North Carolina 9th District ballot fraud (2018)

A GOP operative working for Republican Mark Harris ran an illegal absentee-ballot harvesting scheme. The bipartisan state board unanimously voided the result and ordered a new election.

Sources: NBC News · Fox News · Carolina Journal

Georgia registrations and polling places (2017–18)

While running for governor, Secretary of State Brian Kemp oversaw about 670,000 registration cancellations in 2017 and held 53,000 registrations under "exact match" rules, about 70% from Black applicants. An AJC review found 214 polling places closed statewide since 2012.

Sources: AP, Oct 2018 · AP, Nov 2018 · WABE / APM Reports

Ohio voter purges (2018)

The Supreme Court upheld 5–4 Ohio's practice of starting removal for people who skip voting; the Trump DOJ switched sides to support Ohio.

Sources: National Constitution Center · ACLU · Brennan Center

Related court ruling. In Louisiana v. Callais (April 2026, 6–3), the Supreme Court narrowed Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act so plaintiffs must show a strong inference of intentional discrimination. Critics say it frees states to dilute minority votes; supporters say it ends race-based districting. National Constitution Center · City Journal (supportive) · Campaign Legal Center (critical)

Election interference from Democrats

The Democratic record since 2017 centers on aggressive gerrymanders (three struck down by courts) and on keeping rivals off the ballot, including Trump himself in 2023–24. These cases are well documented and fair to concede; none involves purging voters or cutting polling access. Newest first.

Virginia redistricting amendment (2025–26)

Democrats asked voters to let the legislature bypass the bipartisan commission Virginians created in 2020, aiming to shift the delegation from 6–5 to about 10–1. It passed with about 51%, then the Virginia Supreme Court struck it down 4–3 on procedural grounds.

Sources: NPR via WHRO · Bloomberg Government · Stateline · JURIST

California Proposition 50 (2025)

Voters approved replacing the independent commission's map with a legislature-drawn one designed to net Democrats about five seats. Supporters called it a response to Texas; opponents, including Arnold Schwarzenegger, called it gerrymandering.

Sources: Axios · CBS News · CalMatters · Daily Journal

Third-party ballot challenges (2024)

Democrats challenged ballot access for RFK Jr., Cornel West, Jill Stein and others in several states. In North Carolina, the board's three Democrats blocked two parties despite confirmed valid signatures.

Sources: AP · Fox News · The Federalist · Current Affairs

Removing Trump from the ballot (2023–24)

Colorado's Supreme Court (in a suit brought by voters) and Maine's Democratic secretary of state removed Trump under the 14th Amendment's insurrection clause. The U.S. Supreme Court reversed 9–0.

Sources: CBS News · Manhattan Institute · KDVR

Boosting GOP primary candidates (2022)

Democratic groups spent tens of millions promoting election-denying Republicans in primaries to face weaker opponents, including in Pennsylvania and Illinois. Legal, but criticized from both left and right.

Sources: NPR · Reason · Jacobin

New York congressional map (2022)

State courts struck down the Democratic legislature's map as an unconstitutional partisan gerrymander; a court-appointed expert drew the replacement.

Sources: League of Women Voters · Federalist Society · Hoover Institution

Maryland congressional map (2022)

A judge struck down the Democratic map as extreme partisan gerrymandering; it had targeted the state's only Republican-held seat.

Sources: AP via NBC News · Fox News

Illinois congressional map (2021)

Democrats drew a map to move from 13–5 to 14–3 despite losing a seat. Analysts widely called it among the most extreme gerrymanders in the country; it was not struck down.

Sources: AP via Christian Science Monitor · Capitol Fax

Pandemic-era rule changes and private funding (2020)

At Democrats' request, Pennsylvania's Supreme Court extended the mail-ballot deadline by three days; Republicans argued only the legislature could change it. Separately, $350 million from Mark Zuckerberg funded election offices nationwide through a nonprofit; about two dozen states later banned private election funding. These changes expanded access rather than restricting it, but critics say they bypassed legislatures.

Sources: AP via Fox 29 · NPR via NHPR · Daily Signal

Not accepting losses (2017–18)

In January 2017, House Democrats objected to Trump's electoral votes from about 10 states; no senator joined, so the objections failed. In 2018, Stacey Abrams acknowledged Kemp would be certified governor but refused to concede, calling the election unfair.

Sources: CNBC · Washington Times · NPR · Washington Free Beacon

Fair context. The 2025–26 Democratic redraws (CA, VA) were explicit responses to the Trump-requested Texas map; the 2021–22 maps (IL, MD, NY) were not. In a 2025 Bright Line Watch survey, 92% of political scientists rated the Texas redraw a threat to democracy, but they were split on California's response (Dartmouth).

Scholarly and nonpartisan research

Peer-reviewed work finds voter fraud rare and the 2020 fraud claims baseless, but it also finds strict voter ID does not measurably suppress turnout, so it cuts against talking points on both sides. Each entry notes where the author or institution sits politically.

2020 fraud claims — Eggers, Garro and Grimmer (PNAS, 2021)

Researchers at the University of Chicago and Stanford tested the major statistical claims of 2020 fraud (Dominion machines, turnout spikes, bellwether counties) and found none held up. Grimmer is also a senior fellow at the conservative Hoover Institution.

Sources: Paper · Stanford interview

Voter ID laws — Cantoni and Pons (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2021)

Using 1.6 billion voter records from 2008–2018, economists at Bologna and Harvard found strict ID laws had no measurable effect on turnout for any group and no effect on fraud, actual or perceived. Heritage's Hans von Spakovsky disputed the fraud finding.

Sources: NBER · Daily Signal response

Voter purges — Brennan Center (2018)

The left-leaning NYU think tank estimated 2 million more people were purged in 2012–16 in jurisdictions freed from federal preclearance than would have been at other places' rates.

Source: Brennan Center

Georgia SB 202 voter survey — UGA researchers (2024)

Georgia voters reported very positive 2022 voting experiences, and a plurality said the law raised their confidence. A finding favorable to the Republican law.

Source: MIT Election Data & Science Lab (PDF)

Florida felon voting — Daniel A. Smith, University of Florida (2019)

In a study for plaintiffs challenging the law, Smith estimated about 82% of affected people owed fines or fees that had to be paid before they could vote.

Source: Palm Beach Commercial

Expert ratings of U.S. democracy — Bright Line Watch (July 2026)

Political scientists from Dartmouth, Chicago, Rochester and Yale survey 568 experts and 2,750 members of the public. Experts rate U.S. democracy 60 out of 100, up slightly from earlier 2026 lows; the authors adjust for experts' past pessimism.

Source: Bright Line Watch

Global democracy index — V-Dem Institute (2026)

The University of Gothenburg project, drawing on 4,200+ country experts, reclassified the U.S. from a "liberal" to an "electoral" democracy, the first such downgrade in over 50 years.

Sources: V-Dem reports · Newsweek summary

Proven fraud cases — Heritage Foundation database

The conservative think tank had catalogued 1,177 proven fraud cases spanning decades as of 2019: real, but rare relative to hundreds of millions of ballots.

Source: Daily Signal

On the Russia/Venezuela comparison

Russia and Venezuela still hold elections; what makes them authoritarian is that incumbents tilt the field — they control who runs elections, keep strong opponents off the ballot, pressure the press, and refuse to accept losses. That gives a party-neutral test to apply to everything above.

  • Controlling election administration. Georgia's SB 202 moved the State Election Board chair to the legislature; DOJ is seeking every state's full voter file. Nothing in the Democratic record above matches these in scale.
  • Keeping opponents off the ballot. This is the Democrats' weakest point: the Trump ballot removals in Colorado and Maine and the North Carolina third-party blocks fit this pattern. Courts reversed them, which is how the system is supposed to work.
  • Refusing to accept a loss. The 2020 effort to reverse Georgia's certified result and the alternate-elector slates are the single closest match to the authoritarian playbook in this period. Democrats' 2017 elector objections and Abrams's non-concession are smaller versions; neither sought to change a certified result.
  • Rigging district lines. Both parties do it. The 2025–26 mid-decade wave started in Texas at the president's request, and Democrats answered in California and Virginia.

The two main expert indexes, V-Dem and Bright Line Watch, both show U.S. democracy declining, with V-Dem pointing mainly to executive-branch actions since 2025: pressure on the press, weaker checks from Congress. Conservatives often note that political scientists lean left; Bright Line Watch publishes its partisan breakdown and corrects for expert pessimism, and the Stanford fraud study and the voter-ID study above came from scholars a conservative reader may find more credible.

A question that may land better than a verdict: which of these specific actions would you object to if the other party did it?
