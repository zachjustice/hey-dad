Election Fraud in the United States

October 24, 2026 · United States

Scope. This review examines documented fraud, the scale of the problem, and major allegations about the 2020 presidential election, with one more recent example. “Fraud” means intentional wrongdoing; administrative errors, unverified allegations, and legal disputes are not automatically fraud. Source labels describe general editorial or institutional perspective, not whether a particular finding is true.

Articles and primary-source findings

1. Fox News — Attorney General Barr on federal investigations

Source perspective: Right-leaning outlet (straight-news report) | December 1, 2020

Read original source ↗

Fox News reported that Trump-appointed Attorney General William Barr said the Justice Department and FBI had investigated complaints but had not found widespread fraud sufficient to change the 2020 result. This does not mean no wrongdoing occurred; it means the investigation had not substantiated claims of election-changing fraud.

2. Heritage Foundation — About the Election Fraud Database

Source perspective: Right / conservative | Undated, accessed October 2026

Read original source ↗

Heritage documents specific proven instances of election-related wrongdoing, demonstrating that fraud is real and can take several forms. Heritage expressly describes its collection as a sampling, not a comprehensive count; it therefore cannot establish a national fraud rate or show that any presidential election was stolen.

3. Associated Press — Review of six battleground states

Source perspective: Non-partisan news reporting | December 14, 2021

Read original source ↗

An AP investigation found fewer than 475 potential fraud cases across Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, against a combined Biden margin of 311,257 votes. The cases included allegations rather than solely proven fraud, and even the maximum number identified was far too small to alter those states’ combined outcome.

4. Georgia Secretary of State — Statewide 2020 hand audit

Source perspective: Non-partisan institutional / official record | November 19, 2020

Read original source ↗

Georgia election officials manually tallied roughly five million presidential ballots, confirming that Joe Biden won the state. The audit found ordinary discrepancies between hand and machine counts, but not a discrepancy large enough to change the winner; Georgia subsequently conducted another recount requested by the Trump campaign.

5. U.S. Department of Justice and Homeland Security — Foreign interference assessment

Source perspective: Non-partisan institutional / official record | March 16, 2021

Read original source ↗

A federal interagency assessment found no evidence that foreign governments changed votes, manipulated election results, or compromised ballot integrity in the 2020 federal election. The finding concerns foreign interference with voting infrastructure and results; it does not rule out influence campaigns or unrelated domestic offenses.

6. FactCheck.org — Problems with a noncitizen-voter identification tool

Source perspective: Non-partisan fact-checking (some perceive a center-left lean) | March 30, 2026

Read original source ↗

FactCheck.org reported that federal data matching flagged roughly 10,000 potential noncitizens among approximately 49 million registration records checked, but some flags were later found to involve citizens or registration errors. A flagged registration is not proof of an illegal vote, and the reported figures do not demonstrate widespread noncitizen voting.

7. Brennan Center — Research on voter impersonation

Source perspective: Left / center-left policy organization | Research compilation (2017)

Read original source ↗

The Brennan Center summarized studies finding very low rates of in-person voter impersonation, including estimates of 0.0003%–0.0025% in elections examined in earlier research. These estimates address a narrow type of fraud in specified studies and should not be presented as a measured rate for every form of election fraud nationwide.

Who publishes these sources, and why they are useful

Fox News. A major U.S. news and commentary outlet with a generally right-leaning editorial profile. Its straight-news reporting is directly checkable against the quoted attorney general’s public remarks; news articles should be distinguished from opinion programs.

Heritage Foundation. A conservative policy think tank that compiles named fraud cases and underlying legal outcomes. It is valuable for verifying that individual fraud cases occur, although its advocacy position and explicitly non-exhaustive database limit broad prevalence inferences.

Associated Press (AP). An international news cooperative with established sourcing, corrections and editorial standards. Its battleground-state investigation used election records and local reporting; its findings can be compared with official state tallies.

Georgia Secretary of State. The state office responsible for administering and certifying Georgia elections. Its published audit is primary evidence based on ballot examination, not editorial interpretation; its methodology and statewide results are available to review.

U.S. Department of Justice / Department of Homeland Security. Federal agencies with investigative and election-security responsibilities. Their joint assessment reports official findings from relevant investigations, but the conclusion should be read within the specific scope examined.

FactCheck.org. A nonpartisan fact-checking project of the Annenberg Public Policy Center at the University of Pennsylvania. It gives claim-specific evidence and citations so readers can assess its conclusions; institutional independence does not make any particular article beyond scrutiny.

Brennan Center for Justice. A voting-rights and democracy policy institute at NYU Law, generally associated with center-left positions. Its research is useful for studies and citations, with its policy orientation and the scope of each study explicitly acknowledged.

Conclusion

Election fraud is a legitimate issue to investigate and prosecute, and verified cases exist. However, the available audits, investigations and reported case reviews do not substantiate claims that widespread fraud changed the 2020 presidential election outcome. The soundest approach is to evaluate each allegation against documented ballots, audits, investigations and court findings—rather than treating either isolated cases or unproven allegations as proof of a nationwide conspiracy.
