Immigration

The Evidence-Based Case for Stricter U.S. Immigration Controls

October 24, 2026 · United States · Evidence brief

Claim examined: “The levels of immigration witnessed under Obama and Biden caused significant damage to the United States. Significantly tightening immigration has helped and will help the country.”

Central argument: Immigration can increase national output and federal tax receipts while simultaneously imposing concentrated costs on local taxpayers, housing markets, public services, and some lower-paid workers. The best case for tighter controls is therefore not that immigrants are inherently harmful, but that the pace, composition, legality, and administrative capacity of immigration matter. The evidence below speaks most directly to the 2021–2024 surge, not to an equally damaging surge under Obama.

Articles and Research

  1. Effects of the Surge in Immigration on State and Local Budgets in 2023 ↗ — Congressional Budget Office (June 2025)
    Source bias: Non-partisan. CBO estimated that immigration above historical trends generated $10.1 billion in additional state and local taxes but $19.3 billion in direct costs in 2023—a $9.2 billion net cost, or 0.3% of state and local spending net of federal grants. Education, shelter, and border security accounted for much of the burden, showing why local governments can suffer fiscally even when immigration helps the national economy.
  2. Effects of the Immigration Surge on the Federal Budget and the Economy ↗ — Congressional Budget Office (July 2024)
    Source bias: Non-partisan. CBO projected that the surge would slow wage growth for U.S. workers with 12 or fewer years of education through 2026, reflecting increased competition among workers with similar skills; it also identified upward pressure on housing demand and costs. CBO expected those wage effects to reverse later and projected a substantial net benefit to federal finances, so this supports a distributional and short-term case for limiting the speed of arrivals—not a claim of nationwide economic collapse.
  3. Fiscal Impacts: Accounting for Asylum Seeker Services ↗ — New York City Comptroller (updated fiscal reporting)
    Source bias: Non-partisan (official municipal financial accounting). New York City reports $1.41 billion spent on asylum-seeker shelter and services in fiscal 2023 and $3.70 billion in fiscal 2024. These are real municipal expenditures, not a comprehensive estimate of migrants’ net economic impact, but they demonstrate how a rapid influx can impose substantial and immediate budgetary demands on receiving cities.
  4. Immigration Courts: Actions Needed to Track and Report Noncitizens’ Hearing Appearances ↗ — U.S. Government Accountability Office (December 2024)
    Source bias: Non-partisan. GAO reported an immigration-court backlog of nearly 3.5 million cases as of July 2024 and found that the courts did not systematically track whether respondents attended hearings. A system taking years to resolve cases undermines timely asylum protection and timely removal of people without a legal right to remain—a strong argument for pairing controlled inflows with more judges, faster hearings, and credible enforcement.
  5. ICE Cannot Effectively Monitor the Location and Status of All Unaccompanied Alien Children After Federal Custody ↗ — Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General (March 2025)
    Source bias: Non-partisan (inspector-general oversight). The audit found serious weaknesses in tracking unaccompanied children transferred through federal custody: over 31,000 release addresses were incomplete or unusable, and more than 43,000 children with court notices were recorded as failing to appear by October 2024. These figures do not mean all those children were missing or trafficked; they do support the argument that processing systems were insufficiently robust to reliably protect vulnerable minors and enforce immigration procedures.
  6. The Impact of the “Securing the Border” Rule on Migrant Encounters and Processing ↗ — Congressional Research Service (updated December 2024; publicly reproduced report)
    Source bias: Non-partisan. CRS documented roughly 2.5 million southwest-border enforcement encounters in fiscal 2023, declining to roughly 2.1 million in fiscal 2024, and explained the more restrictive asylum rules implemented under Biden in June 2024. The decline is consistent with the proposition that tighter entry/asylum procedures can help reduce border pressure; encounters are not unique people, and the decline cannot be attributed to one policy alone.

Why These Sources Are Trustworthy

  • Congressional Budget Office (CBO): Congress’s nonpartisan fiscal and economic analysis agency. It publishes quantitative methods, assumptions, and budget estimates; its analyses also report effects that favor immigration, which limits cherry-picking.
  • U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO): Congress’s independent audit and investigative agency. Its findings are grounded in agency records and documented review methods rather than political commentary.
  • Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General (DHS OIG): An oversight body that audits DHS agencies and publicly documents failures and recommended fixes. Its findings should be read carefully without turning monitoring gaps into unsupported claims about individual children.
  • Congressional Research Service (CRS): Nonpartisan policy and legal researchers serving Congress. Its reports explain policy changes and official statistics; the linked report is hosted by an independent public CRS archive.
  • New York City Comptroller: The city’s fiscal oversight office, with direct access to municipal financial records. Its reported spending is credible evidence of costs, though not by itself proof of the overall economic effect of migration.

Conclusion

A skeptical pro-immigration reader should concede that rapid immigration without matching housing supply, local funding, timely legal adjudication, and effective border processing can cause demonstrable harm—especially to city budgets and some lower-wage workers. These findings justify a serious case for more enforceable entry rules, faster asylum decisions, better-funded adjudication, and immigration levels aligned with administrative capacity.

But the broader claim needs qualification: CBO also projects major federal fiscal and economic gains from the recent surge, the evidence here does not establish equivalent damage during the Obama years, and lower border encounters alone do not prove that sweeping immigration cuts improve overall prosperity. The strongest defensible conclusion is better-controlled immigration, not that immigration is inherently a net negative.
