Immigration Under Obama and Biden: What the Evidence Shows

October 24, 2026 · United States · Evidence brief

Central finding: The claim that immigration during the Obama and Biden administrations caused overall significant damage is not supported by the strongest broad economic evidence. Immigration increased the labor force, economic output and tax revenue, while its long-run effects on native-born workers' overall wages and employment have generally been small. But the Biden-era surge imposed real, uneven short-term costs on local governments, and stronger, more orderly border administration is a legitimate policy goal.

Key evidence and articles

  1. Effects of the Immigration Surge on the Federal Budget and the Economy ↗ — Congressional Budget Office (CBO), July 2024 | Bias: Non-partisan. CBO projected that the 2021–2026 immigration surge would increase nominal U.S. GDP by $8.9 trillion cumulatively over 2024–2034 and reduce federal deficits by roughly $900 billion, through higher taxes that outweigh projected increases in federal spending. These are projections, not realized results; they measure aggregate output and the federal budget, not whether every worker or community benefits.
  2. The Economic and Fiscal Consequences of Immigration ↗ — National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017 | Bias: Non-partisan. This comprehensive research review found immigration contributes positively to long-run economic growth, with generally very small long-term effects on native-born workers' overall wages and employment. It also identified plausible costs for less-educated workers and state/local governments, alongside particularly strong tax contributions from the children of immigrants.
  3. U.S. Unauthorized Immigration Total Lowest in a Decade ↗ — Pew Research Center, 2018 | Bias: Non-partisan. Pew estimated that the unauthorized immigrant population fell from about 11.4 million in 2009 to 10.7 million in 2016, contrary to the idea that unauthorized immigration grew continuously under Obama. Pew also found deportations reached a record 433,000 in 2013, illustrating why the Obama and Biden periods should not be treated as identical immigration-policy environments.
  4. Illegal Immigrant Murderers in Texas, 2013–2022 ↗ — Cato Institute, June 2024 | Bias: Libertarian / right-leaning on economic policy. Using Texas records, researchers found a homicide conviction rate of 2.2 per 100,000 among unauthorized immigrants versus 3.0 per 100,000 among native-born Americans in the period studied. The findings challenge blanket assertions that immigration increases violent criminality, though conviction rates in one state cannot establish what happened to every community or rule out specific serious crimes.
  5. Effects of the Surge in Immigration on State and Local Budgets in 2023 ↗ — Congressional Budget Office (CBO), June 2025 | Bias: Non-partisan. CBO estimated that Biden-era immigration increased state and local revenues by $10.1 billion but direct spending by $19.3 billion in 2023, producing a $9.2 billion net cost (about 0.3% of state and local spending, excluding federal grants). This confirms genuine costs for schools, shelter and other services, especially in high-arrival jurisdictions; it does not negate the separately estimated long-run federal and economic benefits.

What the evidence means for the main concerns

  • Jobs and wages: Immigration adds workers and customers. The best broad review finds little effect on native-born employment overall, but some vulnerable workers can face competitive pressure.
  • Taxes and public services: The Biden-era surge was projected to improve the federal fiscal outlook, while imposing measurable state and local costs. Both findings can be true simultaneously.
  • Crime and public safety: Individual immigrant offenders can cause grave harm and law enforcement matters; population-level evidence does not justify assuming immigrants are generally more crime-prone than U.S.-born residents.
  • Border control: A stronger, functioning asylum and immigration system can reduce disorder and allow communities to plan. That is different from claiming that reducing immigration in itself necessarily makes the country wealthier or safer.
  • Obama versus Biden: Obama's years included declining estimated unauthorized-immigrant population figures; Biden's early years saw a much larger arrival surge. Evaluating them as one continuous trend obscures important differences.

About the sources and why they can be trusted

  • Congressional Budget Office (CBO): Congress's nonpartisan fiscal-analysis agency. Its reports publish explicit assumptions and methods and distinguish estimates from historical measurements; projections remain uncertain.
  • National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine: Independent scientific institutions that convene expert panels to synthesize extensive research, including studies with conflicting results and limitations.
  • Pew Research Center: Nonpartisan research organization known for transparent survey and demographic methods; its immigration counts are estimates rather than complete enumerations.
  • Cato Institute: Libertarian think tank that favors freer markets and immigration, so it has an identifiable policy perspective. The cited study is useful because it documents its dataset and methods, but its Texas-based conclusions should be read with that geographic limitation in mind.

Conclusion

The strongest evidence favors a net-positive economic assessment of U.S. immigration, including immigration during the Obama and Biden years—not a claim that every arrival or policy decision was beneficial. Biden-era border pressures and local fiscal costs were real, and immigration rules should be credible and enforceable. But the conclusion that broad immigration reductions necessarily protect American jobs, lower crime, and strengthen public finances goes further than the evidence allows.

Interpretation note: Source bias labels describe institutional orientation, not the accuracy of individual findings. The cited CBO projections were published in 2024 and should not be mistaken for measured outcomes through 2034.
