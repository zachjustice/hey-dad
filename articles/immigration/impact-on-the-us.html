Immigration’s Impact on the U.S.: What the Research Shows

Oct 24, 2026 · @Zach Jusice

Most expert disagreement on immigration is about how much, how fast, and who gets admitted, not whether it helps or hurts overall. This is a review of the most-cited studies from across the political spectrum, not a formal statistical meta-analysis. Each source's lean is noted. No study models truly "unrestricted" immigration; the 2021–2024 surge is the closest real-world test, and several studies below examine it directly.

Context

Southwest border encounters hit a record 2,475,669 in fiscal year 2023, up 4% from FY2022 (House Homeland Security Committee). The flow has since reversed: Brookings (center-left) estimates net migration was negative in 2025, between −295,000 and −10,000, the first time in at least 50 years (ABC News).

Economic growth

The research consensus is that immigration raises long-run economic growth, with short-term pressure on wages, interest rates and housing.

  • National Academies of Sciences, 2016 (nonpartisan, 22-expert panel): the closest thing to a consensus review. Found an overall positive impact on long-run U.S. economic growth.
  • CBO, 2024 (nonpartisan): projected the 2021–2026 surge adds $1.2 trillion in federal revenue over 2024–2034, against $0.3 trillion in added mandatory spending and interest. CBO also expected it to initially slow wage growth and push up interest rates and prices, especially housing.

Taxes and public budgets

This is the real tradeoff: the federal budget gains, state and local budgets lose, and the result depends heavily on immigrants' age and education.

Wages

The overall wage effect on native workers is small; any harm falls mainly on the lowest-skilled, and economists still disagree on its size.

  • National Academies (nonpartisan): long-term effect on native wages and employment overall is very small; negative effects most likely for earlier immigrants and native-born high school dropouts.
  • George Borjas (Harvard; skeptic, often cited by restrictionists): found 1980–2000 immigration cut average native wages 3.2%, and 8.9% for high school dropouts.
  • David Card (Berkeley; Nobel laureate): found immigrants did not lower natives' wages, even for low-skilled workers.

Housing

A Dallas Fed working paper (nonpartisan, preliminary draft) found each 1% increase in unauthorized immigrant workers raised local home prices about 2.2% and rents about 1.4%, without new construction keeping pace (Fox Business). The same study found the influx boosted local employment with little measurable effect on wages. The authors note immigration was not the sole driver of rising housing costs.

Crime

The data consistently show immigrants, including those here illegally, commit crimes at lower rates than native-born citizens.

  • Texas arrest data, 2012–2018 (peer-reviewed, PNAS): compared with undocumented immigrants, U.S.-born citizens were over 2 times as likely to be arrested for violent crimes and over 4 times as likely for property crimes.
  • Cato Institute (libertarian), 2023 incarceration rates per 100,000: native-born 1,221; illegal immigrants 613; legal immigrants 319.
  • CBO, 2025 (nonpartisan): its literature review found immigrants consistently less likely than native-born citizens to be arrested, convicted or incarcerated.

Caveat: most states don't record immigration status, so the evidence leans heavily on Texas data.

Bottom line

Where concerns about uncontrolled immigration have support:

  • Rapid, unscreened surges impose real costs on state and local budgets.
  • They put upward pressure on rents and home prices.
  • Low-skilled arrivals are likely a lifetime fiscal cost; some pro-immigration analysts accept this but dispute its size.
  • Low-wage workers may see some wage pressure, though economists disagree on how much.

Where they don't:

  • The crime data consistently cut against the fear.
  • Overall, immigration is associated with economic growth and federal deficit reduction.
  • Young and skilled immigrants are strongly net positive for public finances.
