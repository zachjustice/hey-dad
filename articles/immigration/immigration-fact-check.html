Fact-Check: Did Immigration Under Obama and Biden Harm the United States, and Does Tightening Immigration Help?

October 24, 2026 · United States · Evidence review

Claim: “The levels of immigration witnessed under Obama and Biden caused significant damage to the United States. Significantly tightening immigration has helped and will help the country.”

Overall verdict: Partly true, but overly broad.

The evidence supports concerns about the unprecedented number of border encounters under Biden, increased costs to state and local governments, pressure on public services, and the effectiveness of tighter border enforcement in reducing unauthorized crossings. However, research does not support the blanket conclusion that immigration under Obama and Biden caused net damage to the United States.

Immigration also expands the workforce, supports economic growth, generates tax revenue, and generally has limited effects on the employment and wages of native-born Americans. Whether stricter policies help depends on which policies are implemented, which immigrants are affected, and which outcomes are measured.

Core beliefs behind the claim

Underlying beliefAssessment
Biden oversaw unusually high unauthorized immigration.Supported. Border encounters and the estimated unauthorized population reached record levels.
Obama was similarly permissive on illegal immigration.Misleading. Obama carried out substantial deportations and prioritized recent border crossers and criminal offenders.
Immigration burdens taxpayers and public services.Partly supported. The recent surge imposed measurable state and local costs, although the projected federal fiscal effect was positive.
Immigrants take jobs and reduce Americans' wages.Mostly unsupported as a general claim. Some lower-wage workers face competition, but broad effects are small or mixed.
Immigrants make the country more dangerous.Generally unsupported. Available population-adjusted evidence does not show higher incarceration rates among immigrants than native-born Americans.
Stricter border enforcement reduces unauthorized crossings.Supported. Crossings declined dramatically following tighter policies, although multiple factors contributed.
Large-scale deportations and restrictions improve the economy.Unproven as a general claim. Some workers benefit, but broader labor shortages, production losses, and fiscal trade-offs can offset those gains.

Articles and research

1. Immigration levels under Obama and Biden

1. U.S. Unauthorized Immigrant Population Reached a Record 14 Million in 2023 ↗ — Pew Research Center, August 2025 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

Pew estimates that the unauthorized immigrant population reached approximately 14 million in 2023, with preliminary evidence of continued growth in 2024 and a decline in 2025. The findings validate concerns about the scale of immigration under Biden, although the estimate includes some people with temporary permission to remain and should not be confused with the number of illegal border crossings.

2. The Obama Record on Deportations: Deporter in Chief or Not? ↗ — Migration Policy Institute, January 2017 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

Obama's administration conducted substantial formal removals, prioritizing recent unauthorized border crossers and immigrants convicted of serious crimes. Although his administration also limited enforcement against certain long-established immigrants, describing his policies as simply encouraging unrestricted immigration is inconsistent with the enforcement record.

2. Government spending, taxpayers, and economic growth

3. Effects of the Surge in Immigration on State and Local Budgets in 2023 ↗ — Congressional Budget Office, June 2025 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

CBO estimated that the immigration surge increased state and local government spending by $19.3 billion and revenues by $10.1 billion in 2023, producing a direct net cost of $9.2 billion. This is strong evidence of real short-term fiscal burdens, particularly for public schools, shelter, and related services, although the net cost represented approximately 0.3% of state and local spending.

4. Effects of the Immigration Surge on the Federal Budget and the Economy ↗ — Congressional Budget Office, July 2024 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

CBO projected that the 2021–2026 immigration surge would increase nominal GDP by $8.9 trillion cumulatively over 2024–2034 and reduce federal deficits by approximately $900 billion, primarily through additional economic activity and tax revenue. These are projections rather than completed outcomes, and the federal estimate excludes significant state and local costs, but they challenge the assertion that the surge was necessarily harmful overall.

5. The Economic and Fiscal Consequences of Immigration ↗ — National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

This extensive research review found that immigration generally increases long-term economic growth while imposing some costs on governments, especially for first-generation immigrants at the state and local levels. It also found that second-generation Americans are among the strongest fiscal contributors, underscoring the importance of distinguishing short-term public costs from long-term economic benefits.

6. The Cost of Illegal Immigration ↗ — Center for Immigration Studies / National Affairs, June 2024 Source bias: Right-leaning, immigration-restrictionist.

Researcher Steven Camarota argues that unauthorized immigrants are generally a net fiscal burden because lower average earnings produce less tax revenue relative to government services used by their households. His analysis provides a substantive restrictionist argument, though its aggregate conclusions depend on accounting assumptions, the treatment of U.S.-born children, and the time horizon considered.

3. Jobs, wages, and the effects of immigration enforcement

7. Measuring the Impact of Immigration Enforcement on Labor Markets ↗ — Congressional Research Service, March 2026 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

A review of seven studies found that earlier immigration enforcement programs were associated with some increases in wages in low-wage labor markets, but mixed outcomes for native-born employment and reductions in employment in certain higher-wage sectors. This supports the possibility that tighter enforcement helps particular American workers while challenging the idea that removing immigrant labor broadly creates equivalent jobs for citizens.

8. Unauthorized Farmworkers in the United States ↗ — Congressional Research Service, August 2025 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

This report examines the agricultural sector's reliance on unauthorized workers and enforcement-related labor concerns. It documents warnings that removing workers can contribute to labor shortages, reduced domestic agricultural output, and higher food prices, while not establishing that every enforcement action produces those outcomes.

4. Crime and public safety

9. Illegal Immigrant Incarceration Rates, 2010–2024 ↗ — Cato Institute, March 2026 Source bias: Libertarian/right-of-center, generally pro-immigration.

Using Census survey microdata, researchers estimated 2024 incarceration rates of 1,195 per 100,000 native-born Americans, 674 per 100,000 unauthorized immigrants, and 303 per 100,000 legal immigrants within the population studied. These estimates undermine claims that immigrants are generally more crime-prone, although incarceration is an imperfect proxy for criminal offending and unauthorized status must be estimated.

5. Does stricter immigration enforcement work?

10. Migrant Encounters at the U.S.-Mexico Border Are at Their Lowest Level in More Than 50 Years ↗ — Pew Research Center, February 2026 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

Border Patrol encounters at the U.S.-Mexico border fell to 237,538 in fiscal year 2025, the lowest annual level since 1970 and far below the more than two million recorded in fiscal year 2023. The decline strongly supports the effectiveness of the broader shift toward tighter border enforcement, especially after January 2025, although encounters are not a direct measure of every successful or attempted entry and the full decline cannot be attributed to one policy.

11. The Impact of the “Securing the Border” Rule on Migrant Encounters and Processing ↗ — Congressional Research Service, updated December 2024 Source bias: Nonpartisan.

Following an exceptionally high level of border encounters, Biden introduced stricter asylum and entry rules effective June 2024, and encounters subsequently declined. This shows that tightening did not begin solely under Trump and that border outcomes must be evaluated across administrations, alongside Mexican enforcement efforts and other changing conditions.

Organizations and why their research is credible

  • Congressional Budget Office (CBO): A nonpartisan congressional agency that publishes economic and fiscal estimates with explicit methods and assumptions. Its forecasts are authoritative but uncertain and should not be mistaken for observed outcomes.
  • Congressional Research Service (CRS): A nonpartisan research service within the Library of Congress that provides evidence-based reports for lawmakers. Its summaries are particularly useful for distinguishing established evidence from policy arguments.
  • Pew Research Center: A nonpartisan research organization known for transparent demographic estimation and survey methodology. Its immigration population estimates incorporate government datasets and adjustments for survey undercounts.
  • National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine: Independent scientific bodies that convene specialist panels to assess published research and evidence. Their consensus reports are especially valuable for evaluating findings across studies rather than relying on a single estimate.
  • Migration Policy Institute (MPI): An independent, nonpartisan migration-policy research organization that analyzes immigration law, enforcement records, and demographic patterns. Its interpretation should be checked against its underlying government data, as with any think tank.
  • Center for Immigration Studies (CIS): A research organization favoring lower immigration levels. It is useful for understanding the strongest fiscal and policy arguments for restriction, but its normative perspective and accounting choices should be assessed critically rather than treated as ideologically neutral.
  • Cato Institute: A libertarian think tank that favors liberalized immigration and limited government. Its analyses can offer a counterpoint to restrictionist arguments, but its policy commitments similarly warrant scrutiny.
  • National Affairs: A right-of-center policy journal publishing extended analyses and arguments. Publication there offers an identifiable ideological perspective, not independent validation of every quantitative conclusion.
  • EveryCRSReport.com: A public archive of Congressional Research Service documents, not the organization that authored those reports. The underlying analysis is CRS research; wherever possible, official Congress.gov copies may also be consulted.

Conclusion

The claim captures real problems but reaches a conclusion broader than the evidence supports. Biden-era immigration substantially increased border-management challenges and short-term state and local costs. Stricter border policies have demonstrably reduced encounters, and targeted enforcement can protect public resources and certain workers.

But Obama's record was substantially more enforcement-oriented than the claim suggests, and immigration has documented economic and federal fiscal benefits alongside its costs. The evidence supports effective border control, timely immigration adjudication, lawful employment systems, and attention to community capacity more clearly than it supports the proposition that sharply reducing immigration of every kind will necessarily make the country safer or more prosperous.

Method note: “Left,” “right,” and “nonpartisan” labels describe editorial or institutional orientation, not the correctness of a particular finding. Border encounters, removals, net migration, and the resident unauthorized population measure different phenomena. The figures in this document are tied to the study periods noted above, not interchangeable tallies.
